I started by wanting to build a world.

So I opened a game engine and started making one: roads, walls, water, light.

I could walk through it. I could move the sun and change the distance between places. But the more I built, the more questions I had.

Who lived here? Who left? Why was this place empty?

I had made somewhere to stand. Now I needed someone to stand there.

Then came Vaidehi.

## Someone inside the world

Vaidehi is nineteen: guarded, practical, and alert to the exits. She notices the ground. She has learned to keep moving because standing still can cost her.

Following her changed how I saw the places I had built.

A wall could trap her. A crowd could hide her. The sea could feed her or pull her under. Every detail acquired a consequence.

That is when *Samhara's Daughter* began to feel alive to me.

The difficult part was letting the world arrive at her pace. I knew its history; she did not. I had to leave room for her to discover things, misunderstand them, and decide what to do.

Slowly.

Even when I wanted to explain everything.

## Read the current

Water came first.

Vaidehi learns that fighting a current head-on can exhaust you. You have to read it, change direction, and find a way through.

I kept returning to that while writing. A scene would get stuck and I would keep pushing at it: more explanation, more sentences, still stuck.

Sometimes the scene needed a different direction.

That is not the same as giving up on it. It means asking what resistance is trying to say. Perhaps the character needs to notice something before she can act. Perhaps the location has not earned its history yet. Perhaps the explanation belongs later, after the reader has a reason to want it.

The world did not need me to prove that I had planned it. It needed to give Vaidehi something real to respond to.

## Care lives in small actions

Then there was Kevat.

He teaches Vaidehi. He bandages her injuries. He makes sure she knows how to survive. Their relationship carries so much in small actions that a speech would feel strange.

Writing them made me pay attention to how people care for one another: what they remember, what they do without being asked, and what they struggle to say.

The same question sharpened the worldbuilding. An invented place cannot become human only through a map, a timeline, or a list of rules. It becomes human when its people have habits, obligations, and fears that change what a door, a meal, a wound, or a stretch of water means.

## Uncertainty can move

Vaidehi made me more patient with uncertainty.

She does not understand her entire past before taking the next step. She has questions and reasons to hesitate. She still has to choose: whom to trust, when to stay, and when to move.

I could let her hesitate without stopping the story. I could let her be capable and still need someone.

That mattered to me. A character does not need total knowledge to have agency. She needs a choice with a cost, and enough of the world in view to make that choice hers.

## Back to the water

I started with the wish to make my own world.

Somewhere along the way, I became more interested in how one person could live inside it: what she would carry, what she would notice, and what she might choose for herself.

There is still more of that world to build.

For now, there is Vaidehi: feet on the rock, listening.

And me, learning to pay attention.
