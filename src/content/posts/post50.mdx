In August 2026, after about five years, I took out an old palette. It had been sitting in the background for a long time, untouched and entirely ignored. It is strange how a simple object can remain perfectly still while carrying the exact weight of the time that has passed. I took it out and looked at it. It was broken. The plastic had cracked along the edges, snapping off in jagged, uneven lines. It was deeply stained, marked by old pigments that had long since dried into hard, unyielding crusts.

It looked neglected. It looked exactly like something that had been put away and abandoned for half a decade. But as I held it, I realized that despite the cracks and the heavy stains, it was still a palette. It was not ruined. It was entirely usable. It just needed to be addressed. I set it down, picked up a wet brush, and decided to clean it.

I pressed the wet bristles into the first section. The old paint was like stone. It had been sitting there for years, locking itself into the plastic wells, turning from a fluid colour into a rigid mass. At first, the water did nothing. The surface of the pigment completely resisted the moisture. I had to press the brush down hard, working the bristles in tight, continuous circles.

Slowly, the friction began to work. The water broke the surface, turning the hardened crust into a thick, heavy liquid. But the paint did not just lift out cleanly. As I scrubbed, the colour smeared. It spilled out of its designated section, bleeding across the white plastic dividers and mixing with the dust. It made the palette look even worse for a moment, a wet, chaotic mess, before the pigment finally wore away under the constant movement of the brush.

I worked my way around the palette. There were the bright colours. The red, the yellow, and the green. I worked the wet brush into them, watching as they smeared across the sections. The bright pigments fought the water for a brief moment before they broke down and wore away. Then I reached the duller colours. The black and the blue. I cleaned each of these sections with the wet brush as well. The heavy, dark pigment smeared heavily across the plastic, resisting the brush before it finally wore away, leaving only faint traces behind.

As I moved from one section to the next, the repetitive motion began to shift something in my head. Each well I cleaned felt like it carried stories. I did not need to remember the exact day I used that blue, or the specific drawing that required that red. I did not need to recall the thoughts I was having when I left that black paint to dry. The physical presence of the pigment was enough. The palette was holding five years of my history right there in the plastic.

Washing it away felt like releasing the past. As I watched the thick, muddy liquid smear and then disappear into the wet rag, I felt a distinct, physical sense of letting go. For a long time, I had been walking around with a heavy brain fog. It was a thick, confusing weight that clouded my thoughts and made everything feel difficult to work through. But as I kept scrubbing the palette, that fog began to thin out.

The physical act of cleaning was cutting through the noise in my head. There was no complex philosophical realization. There was just the friction of the brush, the wetness of the water, and the visual proof of a dirty surface becoming clean. The simple, straightforward mechanics of washing away dirt were actively clearing my mind. The confusion was being replaced by the steady rhythm of a physical task. The more paint I washed away, the more the fog lifted.

Along with this mental clarity came a quiet realization. I was the one who had left the palette in this state. I was the one who had walked away from it, allowing the paint to harden and the dust to settle. I had ignored it for five years. Taking the wet brush to the plastic now was my way of taking responsibility for the palette. I had made the mess, and I was the one cleaning it up. There was a deep, grounded comfort in acknowledging that. I was not avoiding it anymore. I was actively fixing it.

When I finally finished scrubbing the last corner of the last well, I wiped the entire palette dry. I let it sit and looked at it.

It is not new. The years are still entirely visible on its surface. The intensive scrubbing took away the physical layers of dried paint, but the old stains remain set into the plastic. There are faint shadows of the blue and the green that will never wash out. The cracks along the edges are still broken.

You can look at it and see five years of neglect. But it is clean. The wells are open and ready. It is completely usable again.

I took out fresh tubes of paint. I squeezed new colour into the clean, stained wells. I picked up my brush and started to draw.

The process felt incredibly smooth. The surface was familiar, but the space was unburdened by the old crusts. I sat there for a long time, mixing new paint over the faded stains of the old paint, and I made a drawing that I am very proud of.

When I put the brush down, the feeling I was left with was completely different. It was not heavy. It was not clouded by fog. It was beautiful and alive. It was a very quiet, still feeling. It felt exactly like that specific, deliberate moment when a butterfly emerges from a cocoon. It just rests there in the open air, unfolding its wings, ready to exist.
