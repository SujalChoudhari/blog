In August 2026, after about five years, I took out an old palette. It had been sitting untouched for a long time. The plastic had cracked along the edges, snapping off in jagged, uneven lines. It was deeply stained, marked by old pigments that had dried into hard crusts.

It looked neglected, but it was still usable. I set it down, picked up a wet brush, and decided to clean it.

I pressed the wet bristles into the first section. The old paint was like stone. It had been sitting there for years, locking itself into the plastic wells, turning from a fluid colour into a rigid mass. At first, the water did nothing. The surface of the pigment completely resisted the moisture. I had to press the brush down hard, working the bristles in tight, continuous circles.

Slowly, the friction began to work. The water broke the surface, turning the hardened crust into a thick, heavy liquid. But the paint did not just lift out cleanly. As I scrubbed, the colour smeared. It spilled out of its designated section, bleeding across the white plastic dividers and mixing with the dust. It made the palette look even worse for a moment, a wet, chaotic mess, before the pigment finally wore away under the constant movement of the brush.

I worked my way around the palette. There were the bright colours. The red, the yellow, and the green. I worked the wet brush into them, watching as they smeared across the sections. The bright pigments fought the water for a brief moment before they broke down and wore away. Then I reached the duller colours. The black and the blue. I cleaned each of these sections with the wet brush as well. The heavy, dark pigment smeared heavily across the plastic, resisting the brush before it finally wore away, leaving only faint traces behind.

As I moved from one section to the next, the repetitive motion shifted something in my head. I did not need to remember the exact day I used the blue or the drawing that needed the red. The pigment itself was enough. The palette carried five years of history in the plastic.

Watching the muddy liquid smear and disappear into the wet rag gave me a physical sense of letting go. I had been carrying a heavy brain fog, and the friction of the brush, the wetness of the water, and the sight of the plastic becoming clean made my head feel less crowded.

Along with this mental clarity came a quiet realization. I was the one who had left the palette in this state. I was the one who had walked away from it, allowing the paint to harden and the dust to settle. I had ignored it for five years. Taking the wet brush to the plastic now was my way of taking responsibility for the palette. I had made the mess, and I was the one cleaning it up. There was a deep, grounded comfort in acknowledging that. I was not avoiding it anymore. I was actively fixing it.

When I finished scrubbing the last corner of the last well, I wiped the palette dry and looked at it.

It is not new. The years are still entirely visible on its surface. The intensive scrubbing took away the physical layers of dried paint, but the old stains remain set into the plastic. There are faint shadows of the blue and the green that will never wash out. The cracks along the edges are still broken.

You can look at it and see five years of neglect. But it is clean. The wells are open and ready. It is completely usable again.

I took out fresh tubes of paint. I squeezed new colour into the clean, stained wells. I picked up my brush and started to draw.

The process felt incredibly smooth. The surface was familiar, but the space was unburdened by the old crusts. I sat there for a long time, mixing new paint over the faded stains of the old paint, and I made a drawing that I am very proud of.

When I put the brush down, the feeling was different. It was quiet, still, beautiful, and alive. I had made a drawing that I was proud of, and the palette was ready for the next one.
