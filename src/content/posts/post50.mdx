In August 2026, after about five years, I took out an old palette I had been using. It had been sitting quietly, holding onto the remnants of past work. When I held it in my hands again, I noticed the physical toll the years had taken on the material. The plastic was broken at the edges, small cracks running along the sides where it had been dropped or pressed down too hard on a table. The surface was heavily stained, a patchwork of faded pigments that had long since dried into hard, uneven crusts. But despite the damage and the neglect, it was still present. It was not ruined. It was entirely usable, resting right there on the desk, waiting for attention.

I looked at the dried paint resting in the small, recessed wells. The colors were locked in time, hardened into rigid shapes that clung to the plastic. I picked up a wet brush and decided to clean it.

I pressed the wet bristles firmly into the first well. At first, the hardened paint resisted the moisture entirely, standing firm and dry against the brush. But as I kept working the brush in tight, repeated circles, the pigment began to give way. The water reconstituted the old paint into a thick liquid. As I scrubbed, the colour did not just lift off cleanly; it smeared over the neighboring sections, bleeding out from its designated well and spilling across the white plastic dividers. I watched the dark liquid pool and spread out, creating a temporary mess, before it finally wore away under the steady friction of the brush and the constant addition of water.

I moved from well to well, watching the process repeat itself with different textures. There were the bright colours. The red, the yellow, and the green. When the moisture hit them, they bloomed rapidly. The red washed out in bright, saturated streaks, turning into a deep crimson liquid that looked almost like ink. The yellow was much softer, dissolving into a cloudy, pale wash that coated the plastic before being easily wiped away. The green was stubborn, leaving a faint, sickly tint on the surface even after the bulk of the pigment was gone. Watching these bright colours wash out was visually striking. They had a lightness to them, a quickness in how they dissolved and surrendered.

Then I reached the duller colours. The black and the blue. These required far more physical effort. The black had settled deeply into the tight corners of its well, forming a thick, opaque layer that refused to yield to a simple pass of a wet brush. I had to press harder, working the bristles directly into the edges, forcing the moisture to break down the dense, heavy pigment. The blue was just as difficult, clinging to the surface and staining the surrounding areas with an intense, heavy hue that took a lot of effort to finally clear out.

As I worked through these stubborn sections, the physical act of cleaning started to feel heavier in a good way. The old sections felt like they carried stories. I did not need to remember the exact pieces I had painted with that specific blue or that specific red. The physical presence of the paint was enough evidence of the time spent. The palette was holding five years of quiet history in those small plastic wells. Every hardened drop of pigment was a remnant of a decision, a stroke of a brush, a long moment of intense focus in a quiet room.

Cleaning each section felt like releasing something. As I watched the old paint dissolve, smear across the plastic, and eventually wash away entirely, it felt like I was letting part of the past go. There is a specific kind of weight that builds up over five years. It is not always a loud or obvious weight. Sometimes it is just the quiet accumulation of days, the slow buildup of mental residue, much like the dried paint in the corners of the palette. You do not always realize how much is there until you start trying to wash it out.

As the palette grew cleaner, well by well, I noticed a shift in my own head. The repetitive, physical act of scrubbing, rinsing, and wiping felt like it was clearing my mind. The heavy brain fog that had been sitting with me began to thin out. There was no complex revelation, just the steady, simple reality of a physical task being completed. The friction of the brush against the plastic, the sound of the wet bristles, the visual feedback of the dirty surface being replaced by a clean one. It all contributed to a growing sense of internal clarity. The physical act of cleaning the palette was actively reducing the fog in my head, replacing confusion with the straightforward mechanics of washing away dirt.

Alongside this clarity came a quiet sense of responsibility. I was the one who had left the palette in that state. I was the one who had let the paint dry, who had left it to sit for years without maintaining it properly. And now, I was the one taking responsibility for keeping it clean. There was a grounded satisfaction in that realization. It was a simple acknowledgment of cause and effect. You make a mess, you clean it up. You let something fall into disrepair, you take the time to restore it.

When I finally finished the last well, I wiped the entire palette down and set it flat on the desk.

The palette is not new. It is not spotless. The intense scrubbing removed the physical layers of dried paint, but the old stains remain. The plastic is permanently tinted in places, holding faint shadows of the green and the stubborn blue. The cracks along the edges are still there. The years are still entirely visible. You can look at the palette and know exactly how old it is, and you can see that it has been heavily used and left out. But it is clean. The wells are open and empty, ready to hold fresh paint. It is completely usable again.

After setting it up, I squeezed out fresh paint into the clean wells. Working with the palette felt genuinely good. The surface was familiar, the broken edges fitting naturally under my hand just as they used to, but the space for the paint was fresh and unburdened by old crusts. I spent time painting, mixing new colors over the faint stains of the old ones. The process felt smooth and unimpeded, the wet paint gliding over the clean plastic.

I made a drawing that I am proud of. The final piece sat on the desk, the colors crisp and distinct, drawn from a palette that had been restored rather than discarded.

The feeling left behind in the room was not heavy or complicated. It was light, beautiful, and alive. It felt quiet and natural, connected to the very slow, deliberate moment a butterfly emerges from a cocoon, unfolding its wings in the open air, ready to just be.
