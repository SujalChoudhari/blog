# 10 Writing Lessons for Stronger Prose

Good prose is built from small choices about pace, verbs, detail, and emphasis. These ten lessons are tools, not laws. Use the ones that fit the passage in front of you.

### Vary sentence length for rhythm

A run of sentences with the same shape can make even an interesting idea feel flat. Gary Provost makes the point plainly: "Five-word sentences are fine. But too many in a row? They become monotonous." Short sentences can create pressure. Longer ones give the reader room to follow a thought. Let the rhythm change when the meaning changes.

### Start with precise verbs

A more specific verb can make a sentence do more work. "The cat was on the windowsill" tells us where the cat is. "The cat perched on the windowsill" gives us a posture and a little more scene.

That does not make "is" or "was" bad verbs. They are often the clearest choice. Replace them when a more precise verb adds information, not simply because a writing rule says every sentence needs extra energy.

### Use repetition when the pattern matters

Repetition can make an idea stick, but it loses force when every paragraph uses it. Gary Provost's line, "The ear demands some variety," is a useful check. Repeat a word or phrase when the recurrence carries meaning or creates a deliberate rhythm:

> If you want to write, write. If you want to improve, write more. If you want to master it, never stop writing.

### Replace vague description with specific detail

"The food was delicious" gives the reader almost nothing to picture. Bubbling lasagna, browned cheese, and a sharp tomato sauce give the sentence a place and a texture. Specific detail does not mean adding decoration everywhere. Choose the detail that helps the reader see, hear, smell, or understand the scene.

### Cut words that do no work

When a sentence can say the same thing more directly, shorten it. "Due to the fact that it was raining, we canceled the event" becomes "Because it rained, we canceled." Tight writing is not writing with the fewest possible words. It is writing without words that the reader does not need.

### Give sentence endings a reason to land

Readers often remember the last phrase of a sentence, so place emphasis there when the sentence calls for it. "She left without saying goodbye, and it hurt" can become:

> She left. No goodbye. Just hurt.

The fragments work here because the broken rhythm matches the feeling. Do not force every sentence into a dramatic ending.

### Treat the rule of three as an option

Three-part phrasing often feels balanced and memorable, but it is not a rule for every list. Use it when the grouping is meaningful. "Life is short. Art is long. Regret is eternal" has a rhythm that supports the contrast; a random list of three adjectives usually does not.

### Remove weak qualifiers when they weaken the claim

Words such as "very," "really," and "somewhat" can make a sentence less precise. "It was a very big mistake" is weaker than "It was a colossal mistake" when colossal is actually what you mean. Sometimes a qualifier is accurate and should stay. The goal is not maximum confidence; it is the right degree of confidence.

### Use fragments deliberately

Fragments can slow a moment down or put emphasis on a beat. Used rarely, they can shape the reader's pace:

> He hesitated.
>
> Paused.
>
> Then jumped.

The same device becomes distracting if every paragraph is built from fragments. Keep the surrounding prose strong enough to carry the scene.

### Put contrast next to contrast

Opposing ideas gain force when the reader sees them together. The opening of _A Tale of Two Cities_ is memorable for exactly that reason: "It was the best of times. It was the worst of times." Contrast can sharpen an argument or make a character's mixed feelings visible. Use it when the difference matters, not as decoration.

### Use the tools selectively

Sentence rhythm, precise verbs, repetition, detail, tight phrasing, endings, three-part structures, qualifiers, fragments, and contrast all affect how prose feels. They work best when they serve the scene or argument. A passage does not become stronger because it uses every technique at once.
