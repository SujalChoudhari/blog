## Around Diwali, I bought an easel

Sometime around Diwali, I started painting with oils on canvas for the first time. I'd painted with watercolors and enjoyed them, but oil painting always seemed like a bigger undertaking. Even buying the supplies felt more involved. I knew I'd need paints, brushes, and a canvas. Then there was the easel, the wooden palette, the linseed oil, the varnish, and the question of what I was supposed to clean the brushes with.

I spent about 4,000 rupees on the whole setup. I ordered most of it online and got the paint thinner and kerosene separately. I even got a tin for the thinner. With watercolor, I'd been used to setting out a cup of water and getting on with it. Here I was thinking about what kind of container I needed before I'd even put paint on a canvas.

I'd assumed the painting would be the hardest part. So far, I've spent more time thinking about how to clean a brush than worrying about whether I'm ready to put the next stroke down. That was a surprise.

## The pond and the ocean

The closest comparison I have is swimming in a pond and then trying the ocean. From the shore, the ocean looks like it'll be much harder. It's bigger, it moves, and just looking at it can make you think twice about getting in. Then you get into the water and find that you float a little more easily than you expected.

That's how the move from watercolor to oils has felt to me. With watercolor, if I want a light area to stay light, I have to think about it early. I can go over a wash to make it darker, but getting back to the lightness of the paper is much harder. I enjoy watching a wash spread and seeing what happens when two colors meet. I also spend a fair bit of time thinking about where the next stroke should go.

With oils, I can put a color down and decide what I think of it once it's on the canvas. A shadow can look dark enough on the palette and seem too light as soon as I paint the area around it. While everything is still wet, I can change the shadow and look again. I make the first mark sooner because I know I have time to see what it does.

I can see why oils looked intimidating from the outside. I was looking at the supplies and imagining all the things I'd have to get right. Once I started, the thing I liked most was how much room the paint gave me to get something a little wrong and keep going.

## The first two paintings and one very dirty cloth

I started painting before I had my paint thinner. For the first couple of paintings, I used kerosene to thin the paint and wash my brushes. It did the job at the time. If I were buying supplies again, though, I'd get a thinner made for artists' oil paints from the start. For anyone setting up indoors, artist solvents still need ventilation and should be used according to their label. [Gamblin's studio safety guide](https://gamblincolors.com/studio-safety/guideforschools/)

The other thing I underestimated was how quickly a cloth gets dirty. I used one to wipe my brushes, and by the end of a painting I'd used up the clean parts. By the time I wanted to start the next one, I could hardly find a spot I'd want to wipe a clean brush on.

I think I'll try paper towels next. I can fold one over for a fresh spot and see whether there's still color coming off the brush. That matters most when I'm switching from a dark color to a light one. A brush can look clean enough until I pick up the next color and see what was still in it. Paper towels would make that easier to spot. I'd handle used towels with the same care as a rag, especially if they have linseed oil on them. [Gamblin's guide to handling oily rags](https://gamblincolors.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Oil-Painting-in-Educational-Settings.pdf)

## I like painting standing up

I bought the easel because I needed something to hold a canvas. I didn't expect standing up to be one of my favorite parts of painting.

When I'm close to the canvas, whatever I'm working on feels important. I can spend a while trying to get the edge of a shape right because it's the only thing I'm looking at. A couple of steps back, I might barely notice that edge. I might see that a patch of light works better than I thought, or that the darkest part of the painting is pulling my eye somewhere I hadn't intended.

I like being able to walk back and forth like that. Sometimes I step in again because I know exactly what I want to change. Sometimes I realize the detail I was about to add would make very little difference. It's nice to have a way to see that before I spend another ten minutes on it.

## What I want to paint next

I've always felt a little unsure about my drawing. I can spend ages trying to get a shape right. Once I start working with color and shadow, I feel more comfortable. And lately I can see small improvements from one painting to the next. I'm getting better at noticing *what* feels off, which gives me something to try when I go back in with the brush.

Landscapes are where I want to spend more of that time. I like moody scenery, especially when you can see just enough of a place to get a sense of it. How much can stay in shadow? Where does the light need to fall for the scene to make sense? Those are decisions I enjoy making. I'd like to paint more still lifes too. Even with a few ordinary objects, there's plenty to work out just by looking at where the light lands and what happens to the shapes beside it.

I love looking at portraits, but painting a real face up close still feels daunting. Move the corner of someone's mouth a little and you can change their whole expression. Get the spacing between their eyes slightly wrong and they can look like a different person. If I paint people for now, I'd rather show the whole figure. A person standing at the edge of a landscape can change how big the place feels, even when you can barely see their face.

I'll still reach for watercolors. I like watching a wash spread across paper, and there are days when that's exactly what I want to do. The nice thing about having both is that sometimes I'll look at a scene and already know which one I'd like to paint it with. Around Diwali, I was wondering whether I'd be any good at oils. Now I keep looking at the blank canvas boards and thinking about which one I want to use next.
