The same palette has been with me for the last ten years, longer than any friend. I return to it whenever I draw. It is familiar before the first stroke, and it reminds me that the work does not begin from a perfect, empty surface.

I can sit for five or six hours to draw. Drawing taught me patience because it asks me to stay with the work longer than a quick result would require. At some point, the drawing stops feeling like something I need to finish quickly. I am simply still there with it, paying attention to what is taking shape.

Drawing is one of the oldest and most common hobbies. People start, some give up, and some persist. I have stayed with it because it gives my thoughts somewhere to go. I usually have lots of thoughts, and I am not a fan of verbal communication. A drawing that starts from a thought can look strange. I can still stay with it for hours and express what I felt and how I was without forcing the thought into a sentence first.

## The stroke I cannot erase

With every drawing, some strokes are imperfect, almost every time. Not everything is perfect, and pretending otherwise does not help.

An imperfect stroke cannot always be erased. The answer is not always to erase it. I can adapt to it, enhance it, and integrate it into the structure already taking shape. There are multiple chances to respond to a mistake before the drawing is done.

One stroke does not close the whole drawing. I can make another decision around it. I can give it a place in the larger structure instead of treating it as proof that the work is ruined. When imperfect strokes stack together, they can become part of a beautiful painting.

That is different from making a mistake disappear. The mark still belongs to the history of the drawing. It has simply been given a different role.

## Patience is part of the repair

Repair is a different kind of work from erasure. Erasure tries to return to the moment before the mistake. Repair begins after it. It asks me to see what is already there and make the next choice from that position.

This is why patience matters so much to me. If I rush, an imperfect stroke becomes a reason to stop. When I stay with it, I have another chance to respond. The drawing can change without losing everything that came before it.

Patience is not the absence of mistakes. It is the time and attention to keep working with them. Five or six hours at a drawing gives me enough time to notice that an imperfect part is not necessarily an ending. It may just be a part of the structure I have not understood yet.

## Leaving room for people to be wrong

The same lesson follows me outside drawing. I try to give people chances to fix mistakes, and chances to tell me when I am wrong. If someone comments on something I have said or done, I listen. I do not react defensively.

It matters to me that people can speak plainly without having to prepare for an argument. Giving someone room to correct a mistake does not mean the mistake had no effect. It means the mistake is not automatically the final version of the conversation. There is still room to respond to it with care.

Drawing makes that easier for me to understand. A stroke can be imperfect without making the whole painting worthless. A comment can be uncomfortable without being an attack. In both cases, patience creates enough space to look at what happened instead of reacting only to the discomfort of seeing it.

## Building with what is already there

I still notice the imperfect strokes in a drawing. That part has not changed. What has changed is what I think those strokes mean. They do not prove that the drawing failed. They show me where I chose to continue.

The palette has been with me for ten years. It carries time without asking me to start over. I can return to it, sit for five or six hours, and keep building.

My personal verdict is simple: patience is the lesson drawing keeps teaching me. Repair is not erasure. It is learning to build with what already happened.
